Experimental Setup Task, Dataset, and Data Partitioning 3 ¶

The task is per-day classification of smart-meter consumption. Given one query day of readings together with the consumer's preceding reference week, the model emits a single JSON object. The object declares the day original or manipulated and, when manipulated, identifies which of twelve attack mechanisms produced it. The model input is text-serialized numeric data. Each sample contains a metadata line (consumer, month, week, and day identifiers), per-day summary statistics (minimum, maximum, mean, and standard deviation), and calendar features (month, day-of-week, day-of-month). It then carries the raw readings themselves: 96 fifteen-minute values for each of the seven reference days and the query day, 768 numbers in total. No feature engineering beyond this serialization is applied; the model sees what a meter data-management system would store.

7 sentences · mean 18 w

All experiments draw from a single dataset of 8,239 labeled consumer-days spanning 324 consumers and all 13 classes. We partition it once, before any experiment, into a consumer-disjoint 80/20 split (seed 42). The 259 training consumers contribute 6,589 days; the remaining 65 consumers contribute the 1,650 evaluation days. Consumer disjointness matters because per-consumer consumption habits are strong identifiers. A random row-level split would leak consumer identity from train to test and inflate every downstream number. The evaluation slice contains 130 benign days and 1,520 manipulated days (105–130 per attack class), an 11.7:1 imbalance. The split is frozen by a manifest recording the dataset's SHA-256 checksum and every row assignment. The manifest is verified programmatically before each run, so no experiment in this paper saw a different partition.

8 sentences · mean 16 w

Supervision targets are complete JSON objects constructed deterministically from the ground-truth labels. Training loss is computed on the completion only, so no capacity is spent reproducing the 7,900-token numeric prompt. At inference the model generates greedily and its output is parsed back to the same schema.

Experimental Setup Attack Taxonomy 1 ¶

the taxonomy table lists the 13-class taxonomy: the benign class plus twelve synthetic attack mechanisms drawn from the non-technical-loss literature . Each mechanism is a distinct transformation of the daily load profile. The taxonomy spans the practically relevant spectrum, from total meter bypass (attack0) to manipulations that leave total energy unchanged and alter only the profile's shape (attack10). For the generalization experiments of the generalization results (RQ3) we group the twelve mechanisms into four families by manipulation type: zero-suppression, multiplicative or randomized reduction, threshold clipping or subtraction, and shape substitution.

Experimental Setup Models and Fine-Tuning Configuration 2 ¶

We screen seven open-weight instruction-tuned models spanning 4B to 31B parameters and four model families: Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-14B , Phi-4-reasoning (14B) , Ministral-3-14B , Llama-3.1-8B , Gemma-4-E4B (4B effective), and Gemma-4-31B . Every model is fine-tuned with the identical recipe of the recipe table — LoRA in its weight-decomposed variant ; only the base model varies. This single-recipe design is a deliberate trade-off. It makes the screening comparison clean at the risk of disadvantaging models whose optimal recipe differs, and we validate the choice retrospectively in the rank appendix. The 31B model exceeds the memory budget for 16-bit adaptation on a single GPU and is trained with 4-bit quantized adaptation instead; we flag this asymmetry where it matters.

5 sentences · mean 24 w

Two evaluation-side details are worth stating because they shape what the metrics can see. First, adapters are merged into the base weights before inference, so evaluation throughput reflects a deployable artifact rather than adapter-wrapped research code. Second, a generative classifier emits a label, not a score, which would leave threshold-based metrics such as AUC undefined. We therefore recover a continuous score by verbalizer scoring: the output is teacher-forced up to the classification field and the first-token logits of the two class words are renormalized. This restores AUC and enables threshold analysis without altering the generated predictions. Finally, a machine-readable hardware manifest exists only for the legacy 32B pilot run. For the journal-round runs the hardware statement above derives from configuration records rather than per-run capture, a provenance gap we note for completeness.

Experimental Setup Baselines and Reference Bands 1 ¶

Three kinds of comparison appear throughout, and we are careful not to conflate them. The zero-shot proprietary frontier is Gemini 3.1 Pro (0.976 binary balanced accuracy, 0.929 macro-F1), reported on the same consumer-disjoint split as every result in this paper; the parity question of RQ1 is therefore a like-for-like comparison. Refit baselines are classical models trained and evaluated on exactly our split: a random forest, CatBoost, XGBoost, a logistic regression, and an unsupervised local outlier factor, all consuming the same per-day readings the LLMs see. These support paired comparisons. The tree and boosting baselines use seeded library-default hyperparameters; the conference's tuned CatBoost configuration is not recorded in our artifacts, and we state this where it matters. Reference bands are frozen classical numbers from the conference study — CatBoost, XGBoost, random forest, and LOF — obtained on its different, 1,261-row benchmark. Reference-band rows are marked with a dagger (†) in every table, are never bolded, and are never subjected to statistical tests. They contextualize our numbers but cannot be paired with them. This distinction is stated once here and applies to every table that follows. Of the refit baselines, the three tree-family models carry full metrics; the logistic regression and LOF are recorded as balanced accuracy only.

Experimental Setup Evaluation Metrics and Statistical Protocol 4 ¶

Classification metrics : Binary detection is reported as balanced accuracy, F1 on the manipulated class, AUC-ROC (via verbalizer scores), specificity, precision, and recall. Identification is reported as macro-F1, weighted-F1, balanced accuracy, and per-class F1 over the 13 classes. We emphasize specificity alongside recall because the two error types carry asymmetric operational cost. A false positive is a site inspection dispatched to an honest consumer, and at utility scale the inspection budget, not the detection rate, is usually the binding constraint.

4 sentences · mean 20 w

Statistical protocol : All headline metrics carry 95% percentile confidence intervals from a consumer-level cluster bootstrap over the 65 evaluation consumers (B=10,000, seed 42). Resampling consumers rather than days respects the within-consumer correlation of consumption behavior. Paired model comparisons use the same bootstrap on the metric difference plus McNemar's test on the per-day predictions, exact binomial when discordant pairs are fewer than 25, with Holm correction over the seven screening comparisons. Reference-band rows are excluded from all tests, as stated above.

4 sentences · mean 20 w

Explanation metrics : Each day in the dataset carries a reference rationale written by a teacher model under the standard self-taught-reasoning protocol . The teacher diagnoses the day from the readings alone, without the ground-truth label; only where its diagnosis is wrong is the label revealed and the rationale regenerated (the rationalization fallback, about 11% of days). Rationale quality is measured by ROUGE-L against this reference and, more stringently, by parameter-extraction fidelity. For the three attacks whose generation parameters are recoverable from the data (attacks 1, 2, and 4; n=375, about 39% of manipulated evaluation days), we check whether the stated attack parameter is numerically correct. Magnitude estimates (the magnitude results (RQ5)) are scored by mean absolute error against the realized energy-reduction fraction, rank correlation, bucket accuracy, and window intersection-over-union.

5 sentences · mean 26 w

Severity proxy : Several analyses stratify by theft severity using ρ = 1 - E_query/E_reference week, the fraction of expected energy removed, with frozen bucket edges at 10%, 30%, and 60%. The benign day-to-day variation of ρ spans -0.197 to +0.178 (5th to 95th percentile), so the 30% edge sits outside natural variation. Attacks 9 and 10 do not change the day's total energy; they only reshape the consumption profile (median ρ ≈ 0). We call these the energy-neutral pair and report them separately rather than forcing them into severity buckets.

Experimental Setup Experimental Design and Research Questions 1 ¶

The experiments form a funnel. A single screening pass (RQ1) fine-tunes all seven models once under the shared recipe and applies a selection rule that was registered before the screening ran. The flagship is the model with the best macro-F1. The workhorse is the smallest model whose paired-bootstrap 95% confidence interval on the macro-F1 gap to the flagship contains zero, with lower inference cost as the tie-break. Every subsequent experiment — the data-efficiency curve (RQ2), the unseen-attack rotations (RQ3), the rationale distillation and magnitude extensions (RQ5), and the rank validation — runs on the workhorse only. The severity analysis (RQ4) and the deployment frontier (RQ6) require no new training; they are computed from the predictions of the runs already made. Pre-registering the rule matters because it removes the selection of the downstream model from our own discretion. The screening result licenses the cut, not our preference. Results and Discussion Screening and Model Selection (RQ1) 4 ¶

This experiment establishes the premise of the paper: whether fine-tuned open-weight models at practical scales reach the zero-shot proprietary frontier that motivated this work. the main results table reports all seven models in both their zero-shot and fine-tuned conditions on the 1,650-day evaluation slice, against the refit baselines and the frozen conference bands. the scaling figure visualizes the fine-tuning lift per model.

3 sentences · mean 21 w

The answer to RQ1 is affirmative. The best fine-tuned model, Phi-4-reasoning, reaches 0.9331 macro-F1, which exceeds Gemini 3.1 Pro's 0.929 on the same split, and four of the seven models land within 0.016 of it. However, the zero-shot rows show that none of this competence is present before fine-tuning, and the failures are instructive because they are not uniform. Qwen3-8B and Phi-4-reasoning predict original for essentially every day (F1 of 0.0000 at a specificity of 1.0). Llama-3.1-8B and Gemma-4-E4B collapse in the opposite direction and declare nearly every day manipulated (specificity of 0.0231 and 0.0154). Notably, zero-shot Qwen3-8B attains an AUC of 0.9066 while its F1 is exactly zero. The ranking signal is already present in the base model; what is missing is only the decision boundary. This suggests that fine-tuning is not teaching the model to see theft so much as teaching it where to draw the line, an interpretation the data-efficiency curve of the data-efficiency results (RQ2) will support directly. The single genuine zero-shot competitor is Gemma-4-31B at 0.5927 macro-F1. Yet the same model, fine-tuned, is the worst of the seven at 0.8517, receiving the smallest lift from adaptation — though this model is also the only one adapted in 4-bit, so its poor showing conflates scale with quantization and we do not read a scale conclusion from it. The converse case carries no such confound: Phi-4-reasoning enters with the weakest zero-shot signal of all (a verbalizer AUC of 0.5863, barely above chance) and leaves as the flagship. In other words, zero-shot competence and fine-tuned competence are different properties, and neither predicts the other in either direction.

12 sentences · mean 22 w

the selection-rule table applies the pre-registered rule. Two models are statistically tied with the flagship, and the smaller of them, Qwen3-8B, becomes the workhorse. It trails the flagship by 0.0102 macro-F1 with a 95% confidence interval of [-0.0225, +0.0021] that contains zero. One anomaly deserves a sentence: Qwen3-14B trails its own 8B sibling by 0.0056 macro-F1 and is not tied with the flagship. Within this family, scale did not buy identification quality under the shared recipe. Per-class results (the per-class heatmap; full table in the per-class appendix) show the same two classes limiting every model. These are attack1, the uniform multiplicative reduction that most resembles a frugal consumer, and attack12, the consumption-profile substitution, with per-class F1 as low as 0.78 and 0.81 for the workhorse against a near-perfect 1.00 on attack0 and attack13. A rank sweep around the shared recipe (the rank appendix) finds r=8 statistically tied with the default and r=32 significantly worse, so the screening was not capacity-limited and the single-recipe design was fair.

8 sentences · mean 21 w

One number in the main results table, however, disciplines every claim that follows. The refit random forest reaches 0.9122 macro-F1 on the identical split, from the same readings, at effectively zero inference cost; CatBoost and XGBoost refits land just below it (0.9093 and 0.9076), so the forest is the classical ceiling here and the tests that follow are against the strongest classical baseline. Paired on the same days, the workhorse's identification advantage over it is not statistically significant (Δ = +0.0107, 95% CI [-0.0055, +0.0265]; McNemar p = 0.204); only the flagship separates (Δ = +0.0209, CI [+0.0051, +0.0368], p = 0.0095), and it does so against all three classical refits (p ≤ 0.0037 for CatBoost and XGBoost). What the workhorse does hold over the forest is binary detection: its specificity of 0.892 against the forest's 0.715 more than halves the false-alarm rate (McNemar p < 10^-4), which is fewer wasted site inspections, not a better attack taxonomy. If accuracy were the whole story, fine-tuning an LLM would be difficult to justify, and the remainder of this section should be read as an answer to that objection. In summary, we can conclude that fine-tuned open models at 8–14B parameters match the zero-shot proprietary frontier. The pre-registered rule hands every subsequent experiment to the workhorse, Qwen3-8B — beginning with the question of what the result costs in labels and compute.

Results and Discussion Label and Compute Efficiency (RQ2) 4 ¶

Parity with the frontier is only useful if the labeling cost is payable, since annotated theft days are precisely what utilities lack. This experiment therefore prices the workhorse's result. We retrain it on stratified subsets of 5, 20, and 80 labeled days per class (three data seeds at the two smallest sizes) and compare against the full 6,589-day run. the data-curve table and the data-curve figure report the curve.

4 sentences · mean 17 w

The conclusion is read off the curve as follows. Each row of the data-curve table retrains the workhorse from the same base checkpoint, varying only the number of stratified training days; everything else — recipe, evaluation slice, decoding — is held fixed, so any change in macro-F1 is attributable to the labels alone. Growing the set from 65 to 260 days buys 0.095 macro-F1 (about 0.5 points per 1,000 additional labels at this scale); growing it from 260 to 1,040 buys 0.395 (again about 0.5 per 1,000, sustained over a far larger step); growing it from 1,040 to 6,589 buys only 0.174 (0.03 per 1,000). The marginal value of a label collapses after the third point, which is why we place the elbow between 20 and 80 labels per class: at 1,040 days the model already holds 0.7488, or 81% of the full-data 0.9229. The full run costs 5.23 GPU-hours, roughly \13 at assumed rates, and the 1,040-day point costs \2.07. Label acquisition, not compute, is clearly the binding budget.

6 sentences · mean 28 w

The matched random-forest refits answer the comparison this curve owes the reader, and the answer is one-sided below full data. Trained on the identical subsets, the forest holds 0.7451 macro-F1 at 65 days, 0.8321 at 260, and 0.8855 at 1,040 — against the workhorse's 0.2588, 0.3540, and 0.7488. It also reaches a usable decision boundary immediately (specificity 0.52–0.77 at N≤260, where the workhorse sits near zero). The conclusion is not an artifact of the estimator: CatBoost tracks slightly above the forest below full data (0.7667/0.8624/0.8915 seed means) and XGBoost slightly below it, so every classical variant we tested dominates the workhorse until labels are plentiful. Only at 6,589 days does the workhorse overtake them, and even there the identification gap to the strongest classical model is not statistically significant, as the screening results (RQ1) established; the full-data edge that survives testing is binary specificity. In other words, a utility with few labeled days should train the forest, not the LLM. The case for fine-tuning rests on the full-data regime and on the capabilities the rest of this section establishes. However, the more mechanistically interesting finding is in the binary columns. At N≤260 the model is a near-constant manipulated predictor (specificity 0.008–0.092) even though its AUC has already reached 0.887–0.918. The ranking is learned almost immediately; the decision boundary is what takes thousands of examples. This mirrors the zero-shot observation of the screening results (RQ1) from the opposite direction. It also suggests a practical shortcut we did not pursue: a utility with few labels could combine the early-learned ranking with a calibrated threshold instead of learning the full boundary. In other words, most of the labeled data buys calibration, not perception. The distinction is operational, not statistical — an uncalibrated detector at this specificity would dispatch an inspection to almost every consumer.

14 sentences · mean 22 w

Identification follows the same order, and earlier than expected. At 65 training days the model already names the correct attack on 35.5% of manipulated days — more than four times the 8.3% chance rate — while its detector is still a near-constant; at 260 days the rate reaches 46.0%. The model learns to name attacks before it learns to doubt them, presumably because the benign class is one of thirteen and calibrating against it requires benign contrast the small subsets barely contain. Notably, format is learned before either: every prediction at every training size parses against the output contract, with zero schema violations even at 65 days. This ordering has a deployment reading. The capability the cascade of the frontier results (RQ6) buys from the LLM — adjudicating and naming days a cheap screen has already flagged — is precisely the one that emerges first, so even a label-poor utility could pair a classical screen with a lightly trained adjudicator. In other words, what labeled data buys is, in order, format, then taxonomy, then doubt. In summary, we can conclude that the low-label regime belongs to the classical model, and that the workhorse's parity is purchased with the full 6,589 labeled days and $13 of compute. However, every day evaluated so far exhibits an attack each detector saw in training, an assumption no deployment can rely on, and the next experiment removes it — for both of them.

Results and Discussion Generalization to Unseen Attack Families (RQ3) 5 ¶

Having established that the recipe is affordable, we now remove its most optimistic assumption: that the attack catalog at training time is complete. Real manipulation strategies evolve. We therefore retrain the workhorse four times, each time holding out one entire attack family from the taxonomy table — zero-suppression (R-Z), multiplicative (R-M), threshold (R-T), and shape substitution (R-S) — and evaluate on the held-out attacks plus all 130 benign days. The attack descriptions remain in the prompt catalog, so the rotations also test whether a described-but-never-demonstrated attack can be recognized. the rotation table reports the per-rotation aggregates, the per-attack recall table the recall on each never-seen attack, and the recall-bars figure the same per-attack view graphically.

5 sentences · mean 23 w

Detection transfers remarkably well. Ten of the twelve never-seen attack types are detected at a recall of 0.85 or higher, five of them at 1.000. Specificity on the benign days is preserved in every rotation (0.869–0.938), so the transfer is not bought with false alarms. The two failures are attack4, a low-severity reduction confined to a sub-window (ρ̄ = 0.084, recall 0.437), and attack10, the energy-neutral time reversal (ρ̄ = 0.043, recall 0.269). These two attacks share no family — one is multiplicative, one is shape substitution — but they share a statistical property: both barely move the day's energy. This suggests that what generalizes is not knowledge of attack mechanisms but sensitivity to removed energy. The detection floor is set by the magnitude of the theft, not by its type.

7 sentences · mean 19 w

Identification is the opposite story. Multiclass accuracy on held-out attacks is 0.000 in every rotation except R-Z, where attack2 is partially recovered from its prompt description (precision 1.000, recall 0.292). We note that rotation-level macro-F1 values (0.054–0.102) are an artifact of nine to eleven zero-support classes and should not be read as partial success. Naming an attack, unlike detecting it, evidently requires demonstrations; the prompt description alone recovers a usable signal for only the most distinctive mechanism. For a utility this asymmetry is acceptable. The operational action (inspect) follows from detection, while identification informs the follow-up investigation.

6 sentences · mean 16 w

The same stress test applied to the classical baseline reframes the finding. Refit per rotation on the same reduced catalogs, the random forest also transfers detection (recall 0.798–0.997), but it pays in specificity: 0.715–0.731 on three of the four rotations, against the workhorse's 0.869–0.938. The per-attack pattern is the more instructive part. On attack4 the forest fails harder than the LLM (recall 0.227 against 0.437), so the quiet-theft floor is shared where the energy signal is all there is. On attack10, however, the forest reaches 0.862 where the LLM managed 0.269 — its explicit shape-correlation feature recovers the time reversal — while on attack6 the roles invert (0.754 against 0.992). This suggests that the detection floor has two components: a shared one, set by how little energy the attack moves, and a detector-specific one, set by what each model's representation happens to encode. The pattern is estimator-robust: CatBoost and XGBoost refits reproduce all three signatures (attack4 at 0.286 and 0.235, attack10 at 0.946 and 0.846, attack6 at 0.715 for both; full grid in app:classical_grid). In other words, the classical family and the LLM fail in different places, which is precisely the property a cascade can exploit.

8 sentences · mean 24 w

One further regularity in the rotation table deserves a sentence. The workhorse's specificity barely moves as the training catalog changes — 0.869–0.938 across the four rotations, against 0.892 with the full catalog — so its false-alarm rate is evidently a property of its model of the benign consumer, not of which attacks it has seen. This suggests that catalog updates, including retraining on newly discovered attack types, can be deployed without re-budgeting the inspection workload, since the false-positive side of the detector is anchored in what does not change. In summary, we can conclude that detection survives novel attack families wherever meaningful energy is removed, for both detectors. The LLM's two failures point at a single governing variable, which the next experiment measures directly.

Results and Discussion Severity-Stratified Failure Analysis (RQ4) 2 ¶

The out-of-class failures suggested that removed energy, not attack family, governs detectability. This experiment formalizes that hypothesis by stratifying every model's recall over the severity proxy ρ of the metrics subsection; it requires no new training. the severity table reports recall per severity bucket for the seven screened models and the magnitude variant of the magnitude results (RQ5), with the energy-neutral pair (attacks 9 and 10, which leave the day's total energy unchanged and alter only the profile) reported separately. the severity figure plots the curves. The full table including multiclass accuracy and the low-data runs is in the severity appendix.

5 sentences · mean 20 w

The hypothesis holds with unusual clarity. Every fine-tuned model reaches a recall of 1.000 in the >60% bucket, and the entire quality gradient between models is compressed into the <10% bucket, where the best models hold 0.971 and Gemma-4-31B drops to 0.768. The energy-neutral pair, invisible to any energy-based criterion, is nevertheless detected at 0.954–0.981. This indicates that the models do exploit profile shape and not the energy total alone. For the utility, the reading is direct. Theft large enough to matter financially is detected essentially always, and the residual risk is confined to manipulations that remove less than a tenth of a day's energy — precisely the manipulations whose per-day revenue impact is smallest. However, this finding sharpens rather than settles the objection raised at the end of the screening results (RQ1). The random forest also detects consequential theft, so if detection saturates for every competent model, the case for an LLM must rest on what it produces beyond the flag. In summary, we can conclude that severity, not attack family, is the axis along which detectors fail, and we turn to the capability that classical baselines do not offer at all.

Results and Discussion Rationale Distillation (RQ5) 2 ¶

A flagged day triggers a human decision, and the investigator's first question is not whether but what happened. This experiment asks whether the workhorse can be taught to answer it, and what that costs in classification quality. We fine-tune two variants on the teacher rationales of the metrics subsection under a catalog-free prompt, so the attack knowledge must live in the weights rather than the prompt. R0 is a label-only control; R1 trains on rationale plus label over the full 6,589 days and differs from R0 in nothing else, so any gap between them prices the rationale target itself. the distillation table reports classification and explanation quality; the pre-registered paired contrast is stated in the text.

5 sentences · mean 23 w

Three findings emerge. First, explanations are not free, but they are cheap. Training on rationales costs -0.0159 macro-F1 against the label-only control (95% CI [-0.0284, -0.0037]; McNemar p = 0.0067 on 93 discordant days), a small but statistically significant identification penalty. Binary detection, however, is statistically unchanged (Δ balanced accuracy +0.0029, CI [-0.0266, +0.0320]), so the utility pays nothing in detection to gain an explanation for every flag. Second, the explanations are substantive rather than decorative. R1 reaches a ROUGE-L of 0.454 against the teacher and, on the stricter test, states the numerically correct attack parameter on 85.3% of checkable days. Third, the student's fidelity traces the teacher's own difficulty. R1's overlap with the reference is 0.463 on days the teacher diagnosed unaided but 0.384 on the rationalization-fallback days, where the teacher first erred and was handed the label — and the same ≈0.46-versus-0.38 split recurs in every full-data run we scored. The fallback marks, by construction, the days whose readings least determine the attack, so the student struggles precisely where the teacher did. This suggests the rationale channel transmits not only the teacher's competence but the difficulty structure of the task itself, and it cautions against reading fidelity as a single number: the hard tail is where explanations degrade first. We note two scope limits honestly. The planned LLM-judge assessment of rationale quality was not run; a frozen judging package with a pinned rubric is prepared for external scoring. R0's headline macro-F1 of 0.9444, the highest in this paper, comes from a different recipe than the screening runs and therefore does not enter the model comparison of the screening results (RQ1). An explanation names the mechanism; an inspector planning recovery also needs its size, which is the final capability we test. In summary, we can conclude that mechanism explanations cost 1.6 macro-F1 points and no detection, and we next extend the same output schema from naming the theft to quantifying it.

Results and Discussion Quantifying Theft Magnitude (RQ5) 2 ¶

Extending explanation to quantification, we append a magnitude block to the output schema: the estimated fraction of energy removed and the manipulated time window. The workhorse is fine-tuned to emit it alongside the classification, with ground truth recomputed from the readings via the severity proxy. Two questions matter. The first is whether the estimates are genuine per-day measurements rather than class-conditional averages; the second is whether carrying the extra target degrades classification. the magnitude table answers the first; the pre-registered interference check answers the second.

5 sentences · mean 17 w

The estimates are genuine. The model attains an MAE of 0.0321 on the energy-reduction fraction with a Spearman correlation of 0.976. It beats the class-prior control — the strategy of looking up the average severity of the predicted attack class — by 0.0576 MAE with a confidence interval excluding zero. The same holds against the prior computed from the gold class, so the advantage is not explained by classification accuracy. Localization is similarly strong (window IoU 0.874, boundary error under one 15-minute interval). The model correctly declines to claim magnitude on the energy-neutral attacks (mean claimed ρ of 0.012 against a true 0.043), and the schema is never violated. However, a gradient-boosted regressor reading the same raw values attains an MAE of 0.0012, roughly 27 times lower. For magnitude in isolation, the readings themselves remain the accuracy ceiling and a dedicated regressor the better tool. The pre-registered interference check completes the picture: adding the magnitude target does not degrade classification relative to the plain workhorse (Δ macro-F1 +0.0057, CI [-0.0043, +0.0157]; McNemar p = 1.000 binary, 0.295 multiclass). Set against the rationale results (RQ5), this yields a pattern worth stating: the numerically harder auxiliary task (per-day magnitude regression) costs classification nothing, while the linguistically heavier one (free-text rationales) cost a small but significant 1.6 macro-F1 points. This suggests that the price of an auxiliary target tracks the token mass it adds to the training signal rather than the difficulty of the task — the magnitude block is a handful of tokens, a rationale is a paragraph — and the training losses point the same way (0.014 label-only against 0.42 with rationales). We state this as a reading of two data points, not a law. In other words, the model's value is not any single number but the integrated output — one JSON object that detects the theft, names its mechanism, estimates its size, and locates its window, at no measured cost to the detection it rides on. With the capability set now complete, what remains is to price it. In summary, we can conclude that magnitude estimation is real but ceiling-limited, and the deployment analysis that follows weighs the whole package against its alternatives.

Results and Discussion Cost–Performance Deployment Frontier (RQ6) 2 ¶

The preceding sections established what the fine-tuned detector achieves, tolerates, and produces. This final experiment assembles those results, at assumed prices, into the deployment decision a utility actually faces. the frontier table and the frontier figure place every operating point on a cost–accuracy plane: the refit random forest on CPU, the fine-tuned models on an amortized local H100 (\2.50/h), and the zero-shot API reference (\5 per million tokens, 6,500 tokens per query-day).

3 sentences · mean 24 w

The Pareto front runs random forest → workhorse → flagship, each step buying roughly one macro-F1 point for the next order of cost. Both the API reference and the 31B model sit strictly inside the front. The API costs 4.3 times the workhorse for 0.007 more macro-F1, and the 31B model costs more than the API while identifying worst of all. Against the API, the local workhorse breaks even after 523 query-days, its one-off \13.08 fine-tune being repaid at \0.025 per query-day thereafter. For any utility screening even a modest fleet daily, this is hours, not months. The frontier prices inference only, so the label bill of the data-efficiency results (RQ2) must be read alongside it: every fine-tuned point on the frontier was bought with the same 6,589 labeled days, and a utility willing to accept 81% of the identification quality can enter the frontier at 1,040 labels and \2.07 of training. The random forest consumes the identical labeled set, so at full data the frontier comparison is label-neutral. Below full data it is not: the data-efficiency results (RQ2) showed the forest degrading far more gracefully (0.745 macro-F1 at 65 days against the workhorse's 0.259), so the low-label regime belongs to the classical screen outright. The complementary failure profiles of the generalization results (RQ3) are one more reason the cascade pairs the two detectors rather than choosing between them. The tension carried since the screening results (RQ1) resolves here as a complement rather than a competition. In the cascade, the near-free random forest screens every day and the workhorse adjudicates only flagged ones, retaining 0.9114 macro-F1 at \6.92 per thousand days. More to the point, the cascade attaches a mechanism explanation, a magnitude estimate, and a manipulation window to precisely the days a human will review. The LLM rides on infrastructure sized by the screening rate rather than the fleet, so this operating point scales with the inspection workload the utility already budgets for. However, the cascade's flag rate on this theft-heavy evaluation slice (0.925) is far above what a deployed screening population would produce, so the quoted blended cost is an upper bound on the LLM share. In summary, we can conclude that the deployment argument for fine-tuned open models is not that they beat classical screening on accuracy, which they do only narrowly. It is that at $7.48 per thousand days they convert each flag from a score into an actionable, explained, quantified finding, with the classical screen retained as the free first stage.

Results and Discussion Threats to Validity and Limitations 1 ¶

While these experiments support the conclusions above, certain limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the frozen conference ML bands (CatBoost, XGBoost, LOF) were obtained on a different, 1,261-row benchmark. We treat them strictly as context bands and never test against them; the Gemini frontier, by contrast, is reported on the same split and carries no such caveat. Secondly, all attacks are synthetic manipulations of one real consumption dataset. The taxonomy is standard in the non-technical-loss literature, but real theft need not respect it, and our own out-of-class results show detection degrading precisely where an attack departs from the energy-removal pattern the taxonomy embodies. Thirdly, the classical baselines run at seeded library-default hyperparameters. All three tree-family estimators are fully matched across the full-data, data-size, and rotation grids, but the conference's tuned CatBoost configuration is not recorded in our artifacts, so the classical numbers here are floors rather than tuned ceilings; the logistic regression and LOF remain balanced-accuracy only. Fourthly, the rationale evaluation is bounded by its instruments: ROUGE-L rewards overlap rather than truth, parameter fidelity covers 39% of manipulated days, and the planned LLM-judge assessment was not run. Fifthly, every deployment cost rests on assumed prices (amortized $2.50/h GPU, blended API tokens), and a per-run hardware manifest exists only for the legacy pilot. The frontier's ordering is robust to price changes, but its absolute dollar figures are estimates. Finally, the detection floor identified in the generalization results (RQ3) and the severity results (RQ4) — statistically quiet manipulation — is a structural limitation of consumption-based detection, not of one model. Adversaries aware of it could deliberately operate below the 10% band, at proportionally reduced benefit. Future work will re-score the legacy 32B pilot on this benchmark, obtain external judge scores from the frozen package, and fill the classical grid cells. None of these threatens the funnel's direction, but each would tighten a bound this paper leaves loose.
